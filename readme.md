# [Interference Cancellation] Multi-interference Source Radar Signal Amplitude and Phase Estimation Based on Fully Convolutional Networks [with MATLAB Code]

Radar sensors are increasingly becoming widely used devices on road vehicles, playing a crucial role in autonomous driving and road safety. The widespread adoption of radar sensors increases the opportunity for interference between sensors from different vehicles, resulting in damaged range profiles and range-Doppler plots. To extract the distance and velocity of multiple targets from range-Doppler plots, it is necessary to eliminate interference affecting the distance images of each target. In this paper, we propose a fully convolutional neural network for car radar interference mitigation. To train our network in real scenarios, we introduce a new dataset of real car radar signals with multiple targets and multiple interferers. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to apply weight pruning in the field of car radar, achieving superior results compared to widely used dropout. Although most previous works have successfully estimated the amplitude of car radar signals, we propose a deep learning model that can accurately estimate phase. For example, our new method reduces the phase estimation error by half compared to the commonly used zero-phase technique, from 12.55 degrees to 6.58 degrees. Considering the lack of car radar interference mitigation databases, we release our large-scale dataset as open-source, closely replicating real car scenes under multiple interferer conditions, allowing others to objectively compare their future work in this field.

Since our data samples are now represented as images, we consider convolutional neural networks (CNN) to model the mapping between the input image and the clean range contour. Note that CNN has achieved state-of-the-art results on natural images [35]-[37], medical images [28], and artificial images produced after transforming time-domain signals [38]. Our goal is to obtain a clean distance profile from the STFT of the differentially interfered signal affected by noise and uncorrelated interference. We designed a custom FCN architecture to provide a clean distance profile as output (during training, the FCN must learn to reproduce the true clean distance profile on the ground).
We perform a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) on the time-domain labels to obtain clean distance profiles of the ground truth, and train our network to map the STFT input to the FFT output (calculated as Nx points). As mentioned above, the intuitive choice for our network's input (STFT) and output (FFT) domains is that the spectral content of the interfered-affected beat signal is obscured by noise, making it nearly impossible to detect the target, as shown in Figure 1. The advantage of using the STFT is that even if the signal is influenced by multiple interference sources, there is still a portion of the representation where the target is visible (the fine horizontal lines in the STFT in Figure 1). We empirically test our intuition by training the same network architecture (redesigned for one-dimensional FFT inputs) and found that this approach had convergence issues and unusable results.
C. Neural Network Model
Our goal is to create a neural network that can mitigate RFI and map any given noisy STFT input to a clean FFT output in both amplitude and phase. Therefore, we propose a new FCN architecture that meets these requirements. There are some related works that use STFT as input and give FFT as output, such as [10], but these are not based on deep learning techniques. To the best of our knowledge, there is no deep learning model method that can convert interfered-affected STFT input samples into clean FFT contours.
Our neural architecture's novelty mainly relates to the input and output structures, each structure consisting of three different channel representations. The first and third channels of the input are the real and imaginary parts of the STFT, respectively, while the middle channel is the magnitude of the STFT. According to information theory, the second channel is redundant information that can be mathematically determined from the real and imaginary parts. Our initial FCN model [12] motivated the addition of the STFT magnitude as an input, which successfully used it to predict the magnitude of the FFT. Moreover, the size of the STFT has the most meaningful visual information, which can be considered as an attention map [39], [40], in our case, it is not computed by network computation but provided as input channels. The output follows a similar design in terms of channel count, with the only difference being its spatial dimensions, as described below. Although our network does not directly compute phase, it can calculate it from the real and imaginary parts. We emphasize here that we have tried various architectures to explicitly output the phase of the FFT, for example, having only the magnitude and phase as input channels, but we never achieved convergence. However, it seems that phase can be modeled indirectly. Regarding the magnitude, we can choose to use the intermediate output channel directly predicted by the network or to calculate the magnitude from the real and imaginary parts as a post-processing step. The results are very close, but the former method is slightly better. In summary, we predict the magnitude as the output and calculate the phase from the real and imaginary parts. We also noted that without the magnitude as an input channel of the STFT, our model achieved significantly lower performance (see Table 4). Therefore, seemingly unnecessary magnitude channels are actually very important.

Our architecture, as shown in Figure 3, consists of 10 convolutional (conv) layers divided into four blocks. Each of the first two blocks contains three conv layers, followed by a max-pooling layer. The third block consists of two conv layers and a max-pooling layer, while the last block has the same number of conv layers but no pooling layers. Additionally, except for the last two layers, each conv layer is followed by leaky relu [41]. The number of kernels (filters) per block is established independently for each block. The number of kernels starts at 32 in the first block and increases by 32 with each subsequent block, reaching a maximum of 128 in the last block. Exceptionally, the last conv layer only has 3 kernels to accommodate the required number of output channels. We also reduce the kernel size from 13×13 in the first block to 9×9 in the second block and 5×5 in the third block. For the last conv block, we set the kernel size in the first conv layer to 5×5 and in the last conv layer to 1×1. The conv filters are always applied at step 1, with circular padding to preserve the horizontal dimensions of the activation maps. The size of the pooling filters remains constant at 2 × 1, reducing the size of the activation map on the vertical axis by half only when it's necessary to ensure uniform large sizes for the input activation maps. Only zero padding is added to the max-pooling layer when it's required to ensure uniform large sizes for the input activation maps.

Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce a novel Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) capable of estimating the amplitude and phase of automotive radar signals affected by multiple interference sources. We also present a large-scale radar signal database that can be simulated in real and complex environments. Comparing our FCN model with state-of-the-art methods from several countries in a series of comprehensive experiments, we demonstrate that the proposed FCN provides superior results. Additionally, we release our new dataset to facilitate objective comparisons in future work. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to establish a benchmark dataset for automotive radar interference mitigation with multiple interference sources. In future endeavors, our goal is to modify our FCN or explore model distillation methods to enable real-time processing on low-cost embedded devices. Currently, real-time processing is only feasible on expensive GPUs.
